📖Definition of Socialisation▼

Socialisation is the process through which individuals learn and internalise the norms, values, behaviours, and social skills appropriate to their society. It is through socialisation that a person becomes a functioning member of their community.

In Zimbabwe, socialisation occurs through family, school, church, community, traditional ceremonies, and increasingly through media and technology.

📡ICT Tools — Traditional and Modern▼

Traditional ICT Tools

  • Newspapers — The Herald, NewsDay, The Sunday Mail
  • Radio — ZBC Radio, Star FM, Power FM, Capitalk FM
  • Television — ZTN Prime, ZBC TV
  • Landline telephone, books and libraries

Modern / Digital ICT Tools

  • Mobile phones — Econet, NetOne, Telecel networks
  • Internet, social media — WhatsApp, Facebook, TikTok, X (Twitter)
  • Email, video conferencing (Zoom, Google Meet)
  • Online databases and e-learning platforms
📌 Zimbabwe Example — ICT Tools
  • Zimbabwe has three mobile network operators: Econet Wireless, NetOne, Telecel.
  • EcoCash (Econet) revolutionised mobile money — enabling rural communities to receive urban remittances.
  • ZBC TV broadcasts heritage and cultural programmes that socialise Zimbabweans into national identity.
  • WhatsApp groups are widely used by school PTAs for communication.
🔗Roles of ICTs in Socialisation▼

i) Transmission of Culture and Values

ZBC TV broadcasts traditional ceremonies, cultural festivals, and documentaries. Radio programmes in Shona, Ndebele, and other local languages preserve indigenous culture.

ii) Education and Knowledge Sharing

E-learning platforms and educational broadcasts extend access to learning beyond the classroom.

iii) Communication and Social Interaction

ICTs maintain family relationships despite distance — a student in Mutoko can WhatsApp a grandparent in Bulawayo.

iv) Political Socialisation

Through news media (The Herald, NewsDay), citizens learn about government policies and civic responsibilities.

v) Economic Socialisation

EcoCash and mobile banking teach financial literacy. E-commerce exposes youth to entrepreneurship.

vi) Social Control

Community WhatsApp groups report crime and social misconduct, reinforcing community norms.

⚖️Effects of ICTs on Socialisation▼

Positive Effects

  • Rapid spread of information and access to quality education
  • Preservation of culture through digital archiving of music, oral histories, and ceremonies
  • Breaking geographic barriers — rural communities access same information as urban areas
  • Economic empowerment through mobile money and e-commerce

Negative Effects

  • Exposure to foreign or inappropriate content conflicting with Zimbabwean values
  • Cyberbullying and online harassment among young people
  • Addiction to social media reducing face-to-face family interaction
  • Spread of misinformation (e.g., COVID-19 fake cures in 2020)
  • Loss of indigenous languages as English dominates digital platforms

👨‍👩‍👧Agents of Socialisation▼

Agents of socialisation are the groups and institutions that teach individuals the norms, values, and roles of their society.

Fig 1.1 — Six Agents of Socialisation

1. The Family (Primary Agent)

The family is the most powerful influence on a child's values and behaviour. In Zimbabwe, the extended family (grandparents, aunts, uncles) plays a critical role.

  • Children learn language, respect, and cultural practices at home
  • Grandparents transmit oral traditions, proverbs, and moral values
  • Totem (mutupo) identity is passed from parent to child
📌 Family Socialisation in Zimbabwe
  • A child in a rural Shona household learns to greet elders on their knees (kukotama) from observing parents.
  • Grandmothers tell ngano (folktales) around the evening fire — teaching moral lessons.
  • Children are taught their totem early — shaping their identity and social obligations.

2. The School

  • The National Schools Pledge is recited daily — instilling patriotism
  • Heritage Studies, Shona/Ndebele subjects preserve indigenous identity
  • School rules teach discipline, punctuality, and respect for authority

3. The Church / Religion

  • Churches socialise members into moral values (honesty, charity, respect)
  • Apostolic sects (Mapositori) blend indigenous practices with Christianity

4. Peer Groups

  • As children grow older, peers become increasingly influential
  • Peer groups may reinforce positive values or negative ones (substance abuse, truancy)

🔄Types of Socialisation and the Digital Divide▼
Primary Socialisation
Occurs in early childhood within the family. The child learns basic language, values, and norms that form the foundation of identity.
Secondary Socialisation
Occurs later through school, church, peer groups, and media. Builds on primary socialisation with complex social roles.
Anticipatory Socialisation
Learning norms of a group before joining it — e.g., a student preparing for university life.
Re-socialisation
Abandoning old norms and adopting new ones — e.g., someone converting to a different religion.

The Digital Divide in Zimbabwe

The digital divide is the gap between those with access to ICTs and those without.

  • Urban vs Rural: Urban Zimbabweans have far better internet access than rural communities
  • Rich vs Poor: Smartphones and data costs exclude low-income households
  • Gender gap: Women and girls have lower smartphone access rates than men
  • Age gap: Older Zimbabweans may be excluded from digital socialisation
Policy Point
The Zimbabwean government's Edu-Connect programme aims to connect schools to broadband internet to reduce the educational digital divide. However, rural schools continue to face connectivity challenges.

Impact on the Role of Elders (Vanatete/Obabakazi)

Traditionally, aunts (vanatete in Shona; obabakazi/amakosikazi in Ndebele) were the primary advice-givers for young people on relationships, marriage, and life choices. ICTs have partially displaced this role:

  • Young people now seek advice from internet forums, social media, and influencers rather than aunts
  • Relationship advice comes from Google, YouTube, and WhatsApp group chats
  • The cultural transmission role of aunts is weakening in urban areas
  • Traditional courtship guided by aunts is being replaced by internet dating (Metrodate, Dating Buzz)
📌 Impact of ICTs on Socialisation — Zimbabwe
  • With ICTs, games like chitsvambe, nhodo and hide-and-seek are steadily losing influence — especially in urban areas.
  • Radio stations broadcasting in local languages help maintain cultural socialisation for rural communities.
  • WhatsApp groups coordinate families even when members are separated by distance — maintaining some family bonds.
  • Social media has given young Zimbabweans exposure to foreign cultures, values, and relationship models that conflict with indigenous practices.
🏫ICT and Religious/Community Socialisation▼

How Religious Leaders Use ICTs

  • Churches broadcast sermons and worship services on ZBC TV and community radio
  • WhatsApp church groups share devotional messages, prayer requests, and announcements
  • Live streaming of services on Facebook and YouTube during COVID-19 lockdowns
  • Online tithing and offerings through EcoCash and other mobile money platforms
  • Online Bible study groups connect believers across distances

Why the Internet Is Regarded as Harmful

  • Exposes children to violent and sexually explicit content
  • Online predators use social media to groom and exploit minors
  • Addiction to social media reduces productive time and face-to-face interaction
  • Cyberbullying causes psychological harm, depression, and suicidal tendencies
  • Fake news and misinformation spread rapidly without verification
  • Promotes foreign cultures at the expense of indigenous values
  • Internet dating (Metrodate, Dating Buzz) bypasses traditional courtship controls
Rainmaking Ceremony Among the Tonga
The Tonga people of the Kariba area conduct rainmaking ceremonies using ingungu/ngoma (drums). The drum is beaten rhythmically throughout the ceremony to communicate with ancestral spirits and call for rain. Spirit mediums become possessed and deliver the ancestors' response to the community's request.

💍Indigenous Marriages▼

Monogamy

Union of ONE man and ONE woman — the only form recognised by civil law in Zimbabwe.

Advantages

  • Promotes equality and fairness between partners
  • Easier to raise and provide for children
  • Less household conflict over resources

Disadvantages

  • Family lineage may end if the wife cannot bear children

Polygamy (Polygyny)

One husband with multiple wives — legal under Zimbabwean customary law.

Polygyny
One husband, multiple wives — common in Shona and Ndebele traditions. Legal under customary law.
Polyandry
One wife, multiple husbands — extremely rare; not practised in Zimbabwe.

Advantages of Polygamy

  • Social security for women who cannot find a husband
  • Increases family labour force in agriculture
  • Maintains lineage if first wife is childless

Disadvantages

  • Co-wife jealousy and conflict (mhene/intando)
  • Children may receive unequal resources
  • Economically burdensome on husband

💬Indigenous Wise Sayings▼

Proverbs (Tsumo — Shona / Izaga — Ndebele)

Short, widely accepted sayings expressing a general truth based on common experience.

📌 Zimbabwean Proverbs with Meanings
  • Chara chimwe hachitswanyi inda — One finger cannot kill a louse. Meaning: Teamwork is essential.
  • Ukama igasva hunozadziswa nekudya — Kinship is filled by eating together. Meaning: Relationships must be nurtured.
  • Muromo hauverengerwi mwoyo — The mouth does not speak what the heart thinks. Meaning: People can be deceptive.
  • Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu (Ndebele) — A person is a person through others. Meaning: Ubuntu — we are interdependent.

Idioms (Mirau — Shona)

Idioms have figurative meanings not obvious from the individual words alone.

📌 Zimbabwean Idioms
  • Kupa mhuno (give a nose) — to bribe someone.
  • Kudya mapfiwa (eat embers) — to suffer consequences for wrongdoing.
  • Kurova gusvi (beat dust) — to do something pointlessly.

Significance of Wise Sayings

  • Preserve cultural values and moral codes across generations
  • Teach social norms without direct confrontation — saves face in conflict
  • Promote community cohesion and shared identity

🎊Marriage Ceremony — Step by Step▼

The process of Shona customary marriage is elaborate and involves both families, community members, and often traditional leaders.

Fig 2.1 — The Shona Roora (Bride Price) Process

Stage 1: Kutumira Munyai (Sending the Go-Between)

The groom's family sends a trusted go-between (munyai) — often an aunt (vatete) — to approach the girl's family discreetly.

Stage 2: Nhume (First Official Approach)

Senior relatives formally introduce themselves to the bride's family.

Stage 3: Roora Negotiations

The bride's family lists roora requirements: mombe yeumai (mother's cow), matekenya (door unlocking), chitsva (bride's personal payment), mapfihwa (brothers' payment).

Stage 4: Payment Begins

Initial roora paid — cattle, cash, or goods. Until full roora is paid, children legally belong to the mother's family.

Stage 5: Kuperekedza (Bride Escorted)

The bride is escorted to the groom's homestead by her female relatives. A celebration follows.

Stage 6: Kuroorwa (Bride's Formal Welcome)

The bride is welcomed, given a new family name, and begins learning the customs of her new family.

🦁The Totem System — Identity and Clan Structure▼

What is a Totem?

A totem (mutupo in Shona; isibongo in Ndebele) is a sacred symbol — usually an animal — representing a clan's identity, ancestry, and social obligations.

Functions of the Totem System

  • Identity: Your totem tells people who you are and which clan you belong to
  • Prevents incest: People of the same totem are forbidden from marrying
  • Social respect: People of the same totem are treated as siblings (hama)
  • Ancestral connection: The totem animal is sacred and may not be killed or eaten

💕Musengabere and Marriage Forms▼
Musengabere
A form of marriage where a woman runs away to join a man, usually to escape poverty or parental control. Also called 'elopement.' Criticised because it violates women's rights — it bypasses proper courtship and family consent.
Nharo
A woman leaves her husband and goes to another man — requires payment of damages to first husband.
Chimanda
A woman is exchanged for another woman to settle a dispute or debt between families.
Kupamba / Kuzvarira
A girl is pledged from birth to settle a debt — illegal under the 2013 Constitution.

Why Musengabere is Criticised

  • Violates women's rights — bypasses her right to choose freely
  • Increases risk of female trafficking — women can be lured under false pretences
  • No roora is negotiated — both families miss the bonding process
  • Often associated with teenage pregnancies and school dropout
  • Legal complications — children's status under customary law is uncertain

💖Kukumbira/Kurevera — Formal Marriage in Detail▼

Kukumbira/Kurevera is the most respected and structured form of indigenous Shona marriage — the formal courtship that follows all the proper stages.

The Full Kukumbira Process

  1. Courtship begins: A man and woman decide to enter a marriage agreement
  2. Girl introduces suitor to her aunt (tete): The girl invites the man to her home to meet her vatete (paternal aunt) for preliminary introductions
  3. Exchange of tokens (nduma): After the aunt is satisfied, the couple exchange tokens as evidence of their love and commitment to marriage
  4. Aunt's approval: The vatete gives formal blessing to the union — her approval is crucial
  5. Finding the munyai (go-between): The suitor and his family select a respected elder (man or woman with wisdom of Shona marriage customs) to represent them
  6. Nhume (approach to girl's family): The munyai approaches the girl's family to begin formal negotiations
  7. Roora negotiations: Both families negotiate the bride price
  8. Payment and completion: Roora paid, bride escorted to new home
Bigamy
Under the Marriages Act (Chapter 5.11), a spouse who marries again during a subsisting civil marriage commits the criminal offence of bigamy. To remarry legally, a certified divorce order from the High Court is required first.
🎶Venda Initiation Rites — Vhusha, Tshikanda and Domba▼

The Venda people of Zimbabwe (Matabeleland South and Matabeleland North) practise one of the most elaborate initiation rite systems in Southern Africa. All three phases must be completed before a Venda girl is considered ready for marriage.

Vhusha (Phase 1)
The first phase of Venda girls' initiation. Girls attend immediately after their first menstruation. They are introduced to marriage-related laws and their future roles as wives and mothers.
Tshikanda (Phase 2)
The second phase — secret initiation rites. Girls practise the marriage-related laws introduced during Vhusha. Considerable time is spent in preparation for adult responsibilities.
Domba / The Venda Snake Dance (Phase 3)
The final phase and qualification for Venda customary marriage. A special group dance symbolises the mystical act of sexual communion, conception, the growth of the foetus, and childbirth. Held at the request of the chief towards the end of winter.
📌 Significance of Venda Initiation Rites
  • Domba stresses the value of institutionalised motherhood — with all the checks and balances needed to maintain social stability.
  • Only a girl who has completed all three phases is considered fully prepared for Venda customary marriage.
  • The rites transmit knowledge about adult roles, responsibilities, and sexual ethics from generation to generation.
  • The ceremonies build community bonds between age-mates who go through the process together.
Threats
Modernisation, urbanisation, and Christianity have significantly reduced participation in Vhusha, Tshikanda, and Domba. Many Venda families in cities no longer practise these rites.

🏪The Dare and the Gota — Male Socialisation Spaces▼
Dare (Shona)
The general community meeting place — under a tree or in a designated space. Both men and elders gather to resolve disputes, share news, and discuss community matters. Women may also address the dare in certain contexts.
Gota
The boys' sleeping quarters / initiation space — where boys were transformed into men. Distinct from the dare, the gota was exclusively for male socialisation and the teaching of adult male roles.
Inkundla (Ndebele)
The Ndebele equivalent of the dare — the community gathering and justice space.

Functions of the Dare/Inkundla

  • Resolving community disputes without formal courts
  • Discussing land allocation, farming, and community development
  • Receiving visitors and conducting official village business
  • Elders teaching young men about adult roles and responsibilities
  • Traditional entertainment — storytelling, riddles, and praise poetry
📌 Dare in Modern Zimbabwe
  • Traditional community courts (dare/inkundla) are legally recognised under the Traditional Leaders Act.
  • Chiefs preside over dare courts — handling inheritance, land, and customary law disputes.
  • Urban communities have adapted the dare concept — community meetings chaired by ward councillors.
  • Village Development Committees (VIDCOs) serve a similar function in rural development planning.

🤝Ubuntu/Unhu — Philosophy and Practice▼

What is Ubuntu/Unhu?

Ubuntu (Ndebele) / Unhu (Shona) is the African philosophy that affirms: 'I am because we are.' A person achieves full humanity only through relationships with others.

Core Principles of Ubuntu/Unhu

  • Communal solidarity: The community's wellbeing comes before individual interests
  • Hospitality: Visitors are always welcomed and fed — sending someone away hungry is shameful
  • Collective responsibility: The community raises children together
  • Compassion (tsitsi): Care for the vulnerable — widows, orphans, the sick, the elderly
  • Reconciliation: Disputes resolved through dialogue in the dare/inkundla

Essay Key Point
Ubuntu/Unhu is under threat in modern Zimbabwe not because Zimbabweans have abandoned it, but because urbanisation and economic hardship make it harder to practise. The challenge is to adapt Ubuntu to modern contexts — not abandon it.

⚔The Anglo-Ndebele War of 1893▼

Background

After the colonisation of Mashonaland (1890), the British South Africa Company (BSAC) and Cecil John Rhodes turned their attention to Matabeleland — the richest and most powerful African kingdom in the region. King Lobengula ruled the Ndebele state from his capital, Bulawayo.

Causes of the Anglo-Ndebele War

  • Boundary disputes: Ndebele raids on Shona communities in Mashonaland created conflict with the BSAC, which claimed to protect those communities
  • Greed for land and minerals: Settlers wanted the fertile Matabeleland for farming; gold was believed to lie in the region
  • Jameson's provocation: Dr Leander Starr Jameson used Ndebele cattle raids as a pretext to declare war — 'abuse of women' in Shona areas was another pretext used
  • Rudd Concession (1888): Lobengula signed it not understanding its full implications — it gave BSAC mining rights that they used to justify presence
  • Superior BSAC firepower: The Maxim gun gave the British a decisive military advantage

Course of the War (1893)

  • October 1893 — BSAC forces (led by Jameson) invaded Matabeleland in three columns
  • Battle of Bembesi (November 1893) — decisive battle; Ndebele defeated; Bulawayo captured
  • Lobengula fled northwards and died (probably from smallpox) in January 1894
  • Matabeleland became a BSAC territory — absorbed into Rhodesia

Social and Environmental Challenges

  • Food shortages: Poor harvests after land reform; government forced to import food to feed the nation
  • Floods: Recurring floods in Tsholotsho, Muzarabani, and Gokwe North destroyed roads, bridges, dams, and electric cables; communities had to be airlifted to safety
  • HIV/AIDS pandemic: Life expectancy dropped to 34 years by 2008 — the lowest in the world

Government Social Service Gains After Independence

  • Education: Schools tripled in the 1980s; Zimbabwe achieved 90%+ literacy rate — highest in Africa
  • Housing: Government provided land through City Councils; CABS and other banks offered building loans; ZimAsset housing programme targeted new units
  • Health: Rural health clinics expanded; VHW (Village Health Workers) programme launched

🌧️Rainmaking Ceremonies and Traditional Knowledge▼

Rainmaking Ceremonies (Mukwerera / Intwasa)

Rainmaking ceremonies connect communities to their ancestors, the land, and the natural cycle of seasons. They are a form of living intangible heritage.

  • Held at the start of the agricultural season — October/November — to ask ancestors for rain
  • A spirit medium (svikiro) leads the ceremony, becoming a vessel for ancestral communication
  • Traditional beer (hwahwa) brewed from sorghum is offered to the ancestors
  • Drumming, singing, and clapping accompany the ceremony
📌 Mukwerera in Modern Zimbabwe
  • Mukwerera ceremonies are still held annually in many chieftainships across Mashonaland, Manicaland, and Matabeleland.
  • During severe droughts (e.g., 2019-2020 El Nino drought), communities turned to mukwerera alongside government relief programmes.
  • Some Christian communities blend prayer for rain with mukwerera elements — showing cultural adaptation.

Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK)

  • Seasonal farming calendars: Indigenous knowledge of when to plant (linked to star positions, bird migrations)
  • Medicinal plants: Traditional healers (n'anga) use hundreds of plant species
  • Weather prediction: Reading natural signs (wind direction, animal behaviour)
  • Soil knowledge: Identifying fertile soils; protecting against erosion

📜The Lancaster House Constitution (1980)▼

Fig 6.1 — Zimbabwe's Constitutional History Timeline

Agreed at the Lancaster House Conference, London, 1979 — between the Patriotic Front (ZANU/ZAPU), Zimbabwe-Rhodesia government, and the British government.

Key Provisions

  • Universal suffrage — all adults could vote
  • 20 seats in Parliament reserved for whites for 7 years (minority protection)

Limitations

  • Land clause protected white farmers — prevented rapid redistribution
  • Colonial compromise — not truly 'home-grown'
  • Inadequate protection for women's rights
  • Amended over 19 times between 1980 and 2008

Reasons for a New Constitution

  • Lancaster House was a colonial compromise — not Zimbabweans' own aspirations
  • Needed to address land inequality and gender equity
  • Required presidential term limits and strengthened, independent judiciary
  • Needed devolution of power to provinces
  • Bill of Rights needed significant expansion
📌 Constitution-Making Process
  • COPAC (Constitutional Parliamentary Committee) conducted nationwide consultations 2009-2012.
  • Over 5,000 public meetings held across all provinces.
  • Referendum on 16 March 2013: 94.5% voted YES.
  • Constitution came into force: 22 May 2013.

🗺️Devolution of Power and Provincial Governance▼

What is Devolution?

Devolution is the transfer of powers from central government to lower tiers — in Zimbabwe's case, to Provincial and Metropolitan Councils under Chapter 14 of the 2013 Constitution.

Zimbabwe's 10 Provinces

  • Harare Metropolitan Province and Bulawayo Metropolitan Province
  • Mashonaland Central, East, and West
  • Manicaland and Masvingo
  • Matabeleland North and South
  • Midlands

Objectives of Devolution (Section 264)

  • Give power to the people at local and provincial level
  • Promote self-governance and accountability at grassroots level
  • Ensure equitable sharing of national resources among provinces
  • Facilitate development in all parts of Zimbabwe — not just Harare and Bulawayo

Devolution Fund

The constitution requires at least 5% of national revenues to be devolved annually, used for: road construction, water and sanitation projects, building rural health centres and schools, and supporting local economic development.

Key Challenge
Despite constitutional provisions, implementation of devolution has been slow. Critics argue the central government has been reluctant to release funds and genuinely transfer power to provincial authorities.

▼🗳Zimbabwe Electoral Commission (ZEC) and Electoral Process

Elections are held every 5 years in Zimbabwe. ZEC was established by Act of Parliament in 2004 with 8 commissioners: Chairperson, Deputy Chairperson, Chief Elections Officer, and 5 other commissioners.

The Electoral Process — Step by Step

  1. The President dissolves Parliament
  2. Political parties nominate candidates and submit names to ZEC
  3. ZEC publishes candidate list in the Financial Gazette
  4. Voter registration countrywide (voting age = 18 years)
  5. ZEC designs ballot boxes and allocates polling stations
  6. Campaign period for political parties
  7. International observers notified — SADC, AU, ACP, UN
  8. A voting day is set
  9. ZEC officials, party agents, and ZRP maintain order at polls
  10. Votes are counted, sealed and transported to the Command Centre
  11. ZEC announces results — winner needs 51% of votes
  12. Losing candidate may file a petition within 14 days
  13. If no candidate wins 51%, an election re-run is held

▼👔Indigenous Entitlements — Mombe Yaamai and Benza
📑 Key Shona Custom
These are gifts/entitlements given to parents whose child is being married into another family — a recognition of their role in raising the child.

Entitlements for Women

Mombe Yaamai (Mother's Cow)
A heifer given to the mother whose child is being married. If she has died, her sisters receive it. When the calf grows it is killed by the son-in-law during bride price payment. Common in: Zezuru, Mabuja, Mutoko, Makaranga, Kore Kore, Manicaland.
Tseu Yaamai (Mother's Field)
A small field (1–2 hectares) from the main field given to the mother to cultivate her own crops — millet, groundnuts, rapoko, maize, cowpeas. She can sell or consume the harvest.

Entitlements for Men

Father's Bull (Danga)
A bull given by the son-in-law to acknowledge the father's authority and his role in raising the child.
Lobola
Paid in respect of the daughter — in the form of cattle, cash, majasi, etc.
Benza (Father's Field)
A small field for the father. Common in Kore Kore, Mabuja, Zezuru, Karanga communities.

Distinguish mombe yaamai (always paid, for the mother) from chimanda (only paid if bride was a virgin). Mombe yaamai is a universal entitlement; chimanda is conditional.

🏢Formal Sector — Characteristics▼
Formal Sector
The part of an economy that is regulated, registered, and taxed by government. Workers receive formal employment contracts, pay PAYE, and are protected by the Labour Act.
Informal Sector
Economic activities that are NOT formally registered or regulated — often called the 'grey economy.' Includes street vendors, flea markets, backyard mechanics, and small traders.

Characteristics of the Formal Sector

  • Registered with the Zimbabwe Revenue Authority (ZIMRA) — pays tax
  • Employees receive written contracts, pay slips, and statutory benefits
  • Subject to Labour Act (Chapter 28:01) — minimum wage, leave, dismissal procedures
  • Registered with National Social Security Authority (NSSA) — pension and workers' compensation
  • Use of formal banking — salaries paid through banks; VAT registered
  • Regulated working hours, health and safety standards, and occupational requirements
  • Transparent financial records — audited accounts required

Challenges of the Informal Sector

  • Capital constraints — cannot access bank loans without collateral or formal records
  • No legal protection — not covered by Labour Act or NSSA
  • Eviction risk — street vendors and market traders can be cleared by councils
  • No quality control — substandard goods with no consumer protection
  • Communication network challenges — limited access to market information
  • Exploitation — workers receive no benefits, can be dismissed without notice

▼🍳Indigenous Food Preservation and Storage Methods

🔋 Smoking

Meat, fish, fruits, and milk kept in cool/shaded areas to slow decomposition.

Canning
Sealing food in metal cans — fish, fruits, meat. Airtight seal prevents contamination.
Fermenting
Beer, yoghurt, cheese, wine — controlled fermentation by beneficial bacteria extends shelf life.

▼🌞Land Degradation — Causes, Effects and Prevention

Land degradation is the process by which the value of the biophysical environment is affected by human-induced processes acting upon the land.

  • Construction of dams — water conservation
  • Contour farming — ploughing along natural contours
  • Afforestation — planting trees to restore vegetation cover
  • Government policy enforcement through EMA

▼🦠Pandemics and Chronic Illnesses — Ebola, HIV/AIDS, Cholera, Dysentery

A pandemic is an epidemic disease that spreads across populations over vast areas such as continents.

A) Ebola (Haemorrhagic Fever)

Transmission
Contact with infected wildlife (fruit bats, chimpanzees, gorillas); person-to-person through blood, breast milk, semen, sweat, stool, urine, vomit.
Symptoms
Fever, headache, back aches, diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, rapid weight loss, bleeding from eyes, reduced liver and kidney function.
Prevention
Rapid quarantining; protective clothing; safe burial practices; safe injection practices; regular hand washing; sanitisation.
Impact
16,000+ children orphaned in Guinea, Liberia, Sierra Leone (2015); school closures for 6 months (2014); Christmas cancelled in Sierra Leone; job losses.

B) HIV and AIDS

HIV = Human Immunodeficiency Virus | AIDS = Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome

Transmission
Unprotected sex with infected person; mother-to-child; contact with infected blood; sharing sharp objects.
Symptoms
Weight loss, diarrhoea, swollen lymph nodes, fever, night sweats, fatigue, nail changes, mouth sores, extreme tiredness.
Prevention
Abstinence; avoiding multiple partners; use of condoms; knowing your status; being faithful; use of ARVs for prevention of mother-to-child transmission.
Economic Impact
Reduces human capital; people with AIDS cannot work; increases orphans; shrinks skilled labour force; reduces productivity.

C) Cholera

Causes
Contaminated water or food by bacteria; poor sanitation; poor hygiene; contaminated fruits and vegetables.
Symptoms
Explosive watery diarrhoea; vomiting; leg cramps; severe dehydration (sunken eyes, dry mouth, low blood pressure, fast heartbeat).
Prevention
Eat peeled fruits; avoid salads, raw fish, uncooked vegetables; ensure food is thoroughly cooked; drink boiled or bottled water.
Impact
Human suffering; loss of life; travel restrictions; strains the healthcare system.

D) Dysentery (Intestinal Inflammation)

Causes
Bacillary dysentery from Shigella bacteria in faeces; contaminated food; poor sanitation; using human faeces as fertiliser.
Symptoms
Mild stomach pain; blood in diarrhoea; nausea; vomiting; severe abdominal pain; stomach cramps; high temperature (fever).
Prevention
Wash hands with soap after toilet; before eating; before feeding babies; avoid sharing towels; wash infected person's clothes in hot water; drink boiled water.

Liberation War Heritage Sites

1. Chimoio — Operation Dingo
ZANLA headquarters in Mozambique. Sub-camps: Osibisa (women); Chaminuka (security); Chindunduma (school children); Percy Ntini (logistics); Garage (vehicle workshop); Takawira Base Two (military). Attack by 84 Rhodesian Selous Scouts on 9 August 1976, under Captain Rob Warracker. Morrison Nyathi (a sellout/spy) blew a whistle to assemble unsuspecting combatants. Shot at point-blank range — 1,028 fighters perished. Trucks and uniforms were disguised as ZANLA vehicles.
3. Freedom Camp
A transit camp in Zambia. Rhodesian forces attacked on 28 October 1978 using 6 Canberra bombers, killing about 400 people.

Importance of Liberation Heritage Sites

  • Remind Zimbabweans of how independence was achieved
  • Brutal killings by Rhodesians motivate people to safeguard the gains of independence
  • Appreciate the role played by neighbouring countries in the struggle
  • They are part of Zimbabwe's heritage and national memory
  • Help citizens understand how the liberation struggle was fought

World Heritage Sites

1. Matobo Hills (UNESCO 2003)
3,100 sq km of kopjes and balancing rocks. Highest point: Gulati (1,549m). Nswatugi Cave has stone age rock art — Bambata, Pamongwe, White Rhino, World's View. Malindidzimu summit has the grave of Cecil John Rhodes. Sacred Njelele shrine of Mwari is here.
2. Victoria Falls (UNESCO 1989)
Called Mosi-oa-Tunya by the local Tonga people ("the smoke that thunders"). Largest curtain of falling water — 1,708 metres wide. Heights: Devils Cataract (61m), Main Falls (83m), Rainbow Falls (93m), Eastern Cataract (98m). Produces 500 million litres/minute at peak.
3. Great Zimbabwe (UNESCO 1986)
Capital during the Later Iron Age. At peak: covered 200-mile radius, housed 20,000 people. Built with granite blocks without mortar — walls 9m high and 2m wide. Three sections: Hill Complex (king's residence), Great Enclosure (first wife), Valley Ruins (other wives). 8 soapstone Zimbabwe Birds found — 6 from Hill Complex (national emblem).

Importance of World Heritage Sites

  • Tourist attractions generating foreign currency for Zimbabwe
  • Sites receive funds from UNESCO for protection and conservation
  • Sources of history and culture — educational value
  • Promote SMEs in craft making near the sites
  • Create employment for local communities
  • Facilitate infrastructure development — transport and communication
  • Give the nation its identity and international recognition

📝 Model Essay — Topic 3: Cultural Heritage

Q: (a) Describe the concept of Ubuntu/Unhu [8] (b) Explain the importance of Ubuntu in Zimbabwe [6] (c) Suggest how Ubuntu can be preserved in modern Zimbabwe [6]

a

Describing Ubuntu/Unhu [8 marks]

Ubuntu/Unhu is the African philosophy that affirms 'I am because we are' — a person achieves full humanity only through their relationships with others, not through individual achievement alone. Firstly, Ubuntu is characterised by compassion — the feeling to help one who is suffering or in need, as seen when a community helps a bereaved family with food and support. Secondly, reciprocity is central to Ubuntu — people give back the same kind of treatment they have received, creating a cycle of mutual support. Thirdly, dignity — the state of being worthy of respect — means that Ubuntu demands that every person is treated with honour regardless of social status. Fourthly, humanity — the quality of being humane and kind — is expressed in practices like hospitality, where no visitor leaves a Zimbabwean homestead hungry. Fifthly, mutual interest means having a common purpose within society — as seen in nhimbe, where a community collectively helps a neighbour harvest crops. Sixthly, oneness or unity — expressed in the Ndebele phrase 'umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu' — binds communities into a shared identity. Seventhly, tolerance — the willingness to accept opinions or behaviours one disagrees with — allows diverse communities to coexist. Finally, Ubuntu produces a well-cultured citizen who grows responsibly and places community needs above personal gain.

b

Importance of Ubuntu in Zimbabwe [6 marks]

Firstly, Ubuntu expresses African dignity and shared relations — it gives Zimbabweans a cultural identity that is distinctly African and not borrowed from foreign philosophies. Secondly, Ubuntu builds social cohesion — communities view justice and solidarity as fundamental to survival, reducing crime and conflict at community level. Thirdly, Ubuntu supports the view that people do not live in isolation — this underpins institutions like the dare (community court) and nhimbe (communal labour), which continue to function in rural Zimbabwe. Fourthly, Ubuntu promotes positive thinking and collective responsibility — communities take collective action on problems such as maintaining boreholes or schools, rather than waiting for government. Fifthly, Ubuntu inspired Zimbabwe's greatest political reconciliation — the 1987 Unity Accord between ZANU PF and ZAPU chose dialogue over continued conflict. Finally, Ubuntu promotes oneness across ethnic and cultural divides — in a country with 16 official languages and multiple ethnic groups, Ubuntu philosophy reduces tribal tensions.

c

Preserving Ubuntu in Modern Zimbabwe [6 marks]

Firstly, Heritage Studies must be taught meaningfully in schools — the MoPSE curriculum already includes Ubuntu/Unhu, but teachers should use real Zimbabwe examples so students see its relevance. Secondly, community leaders and traditional authorities should actively practise and model Ubuntu values — a chief who conducts nhimbe for community members demonstrates Ubuntu more powerfully than any textbook. Thirdly, media — including ZBC TV and community radio stations — should broadcast programmes celebrating Ubuntu practices such as burial societies, nhimbe, and community clean-ups. Fourthly, the government should support cultural festivals like HIFA and Intwasa where Ubuntu-based arts, music, and storytelling are celebrated publicly. Fifthly, families should maintain extended family ties — remittances to rural relatives, participation in funeral ceremonies, and teaching children their totem identity all maintain Ubuntu in an urbanising society. Finally, civil society organisations like burial societies (sununguko) and women's clubs should be supported as modern expressions of Ubuntu-based collective action.
